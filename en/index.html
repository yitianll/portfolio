02 / Field notes

Southeast Asia field notes

From Chengdu to Singapore, then out from Singapore to Penang, Chiang Mai, Kuala Lumpur and Johor Bahru. In every city I look at the small shops, the markets and the signs, to learn how people live, what language they speak, and what makes them stop and care.

StudyProjectSingapore

SingaporeHOME BASE · NUS

Singapore is my home in Southeast Asia and my starting point for every trip; across the Causeway is Johor Bahru. Century-old shophouses stand next to HDB towers, gift shops sell hawker-centre and Peranakan-tile souvenirs, and junglefowl stroll across the campus lawns.

At NUS we judge a place by more than its buildings: social and cultural life, economics and technology, ecology and climate all count. This year I also redesigned International Plaza in Tanjong Pagar with a classmate.

→ WHAT IT TAUGHT ME

A space or a community rarely loses energy because too few people come. It loses energy because it was built for one kind of person and one use. Giving more people a reason to take part matters more than pulling in traffic.

TravelMalaysia

PenangGEORGE TOWN

In George Town I photographed shops more than sights: handwoven cloth, plants, second-hand books, independent design. A hundred-year-old shophouse can be a café at the front and a studio at the back. Each generation reuses the space, and the street keeps its character.

→ WHAT IT TAUGHT ME

Local creators often run these small shops; the owner is the content. When starting in a new market, look first for people with a shop, a point of view and a loyal crowd, before chasing big follower counts.

TravelThailand

Chiang MaiNORTHERN THAILAND

The markets, concept stores and textile museum in Chiang Mai showed me that craft and slow living aren't a style here; they're daily life. Many stallholders arrange and photograph their work with care. The shop itself is a content channel.

→ WHAT IT TAUGHT ME

Craft, coffee, slow travel and digital-nomad life are natural fits for discovery-style content. Chiang Mai's makers and nomad communities are the kind of small seed groups a new content product can start with.

TravelMalaysia

Kuala LumpurMALAYSIA

One KL street can carry Chinese shop signs, Malay road signs and English menus, with a fruit stall right under a skyscraper. Chinese, Malay and Indian communities each have their own languages and content habits, and they overlap in the same city.

→ WHAT IT TAUGHT ME

Southeast Asia isn't one market. It's many combinations of community and language. Bilingual creators, like Malaysian Chinese creators who know both Chinese-language content and local life, are natural bridges between audiences.

ProjectRemote · not visitedIndonesia

SurabayaLIVING ROOM PROJECT

Surabaya Living Room, a railway station redesign. I didn't travel to Surabaya: teammates went on site and brought back photos and notes, and we researched the rest together. Instead of dropping in a generic modern plaza, we started from how Indonesians already gather outdoors: alun-alun (town square), panggung (stage) and pendopo (open roofed pavilion).

→ WHAT IT TAUGHT ME

You can't visit every market yourself. First-hand material from local partners, plus structured research, still leads to decisions that fit local life. And designing an onboarding flow is like designing a station entrance: use the spatial language people already know, so newcomers can tell where to go, where to pause and whom they'll meet.

ISD5105 · District study · Team of five

ChinatownOne street, three groups, three ways of using it

Chinatown is one of Singapore's oldest conservation districts: two- and three-storey shophouses, narrow streets, temples and hawker centres packed together. We split users into residents, tourists, and shopkeepers and vendors, and recorded where each group goes, what they do and how long they stay.

  • Planned activities: shopping, eating, worship and photos, concentrated on Pagoda Street, Smith Street and around the temples.
  • Incidental activities: resting under the five-foot way, sheltering from the sun, watching the street, chatting with neighbours. Most of the sense of community lives here.
  • Three rhythms a day: elderly residents walking and playing chess in the morning; workers and tourists peaking at midday; food bringing the streets back to life at night.
  • Three problems: five-foot ways blocked by goods, hot and dirty back lanes, and alleys that feel unsafe at night. Our proposals: clear walking space, clean back lanes, separate the flows.
→ Takeaway for content & community

The same place serves very different needs. Content and community work the same way: define the audiences first, then design for each moment and context, rather than one programme for everyone.

ISD5105 · HDB town study · Team of five

Ang Mo KioThe busiest space isn't the biggest one

Ang Mo Kio is one of Singapore's earliest HDB new towns. We started with how services are spread across the town, zoomed in to 300 m and 600 m walking radii, then picked four observation points, A to D, around the central market and hawker centre, counting, photographing and drawing sections at 9, 12, 14 and 18h on a weekday and a weekend.

People counted per observation slot; outlined cells mark the busiest point in each slot. Source: our team's field counts.

  • Four points, one daily route: A is for passing through, B is for a short pause, C is where people eat and meet, D is just on the way.
  • Bigger isn't busier: D, the largest space, stayed quiet; B and C, with shade, seats, shopfronts and clear paths, were busy all day.
  • Weekdays and weekends are two different lives: weekdays follow commutes and lunch; weekends belong to families, older residents and grocery runs.
→ Takeaway for content & community

A community's energy comes from a chain of small, useful touchpoints. Rather than one big plaza, design several small reasons to stop: a recurring topic, a small event, a regular column.

Indonesia · ISD5101 · 2025

Surabaya Living Room
Railway station redesign

NUS Integrated Sustainable Design · Team of three (Li Jiatai / Luo Yitian / Yang Jiayi) · Site photos by teammates who visited

The old station was just a station building and a few kiosks surrounded by open-air parking. We reframed it as the city's living room: easy to reach, able to host many activities, pleasant to be in, and connected to its surroundings.

alun-alunTown square → family activities and open lawns
panggungStage → event decks and viewing platforms
pendopoOpen pavilion → shaded semi-outdoor gathering spots
  • Single use→Station + night / day market + job fairs + pop-up exhibitions
  • Exclusion→Less surface parking, soft edges that welcome passers-by
  • Car-first→Green corridors and a ground-level walking network
  • Isolated→Corridors linking nearby hospitals and malls
→ Takeaway for content & community

Find the ways people already gather, then add to them, instead of importing a template. Launching a community works the same way: build on existing circles and rituals.

Singapore · ISD5102 · 2026

RegenCBD
International Plaza
retrofit

NUS Integrated Sustainable Design · Team of two (Luo Yitian / Huang Jingyuan)

Hybrid work has cut demand for CBD offices worldwide, and single-use towers feel it most. We took International Plaza in Tanjong Pagar and, in line with Singapore's CBD Incentive Scheme, turned an office block that empties after work into a vertical neighbourhood of homes, offices, shops and community space.

+35%gross floor area
-32%lifecycle carbon
6assessment dimensions
  • Offices only→Homes, kindergarten, supermarket, clinic, co-working
  • Poor MRT link→Better connections to the MRT and walking network
  • No social space→Sky gardens, pool, sports court, rooftop bar
  • Demolish→Keep the structure, add mass timber, cut embodied carbon
→ Takeaway for content & community

A community stays active when different people have different reasons to come back at different times. Growth paths for creators or members work the same way: newcomers, regulars and top contributors each need their own floor and their own rewards.
